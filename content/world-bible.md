## Module 1: The Heart

**Heart Statement: If you had everything you ever wanted, would you still want the truth?**

This is a compass, not a tattoo. It can sharpen as the world grows.

### The message of the whole story

**Free will exists, but our minds have always put restraints on us.** Society, sunk costs, expectations and beliefs cage people, and that pattern repeats unless someone grows up with their mind nurtured, told they can be whatever they want, until their reality mimics it.

- The goal for the ending and the true realisation: the reader closes the series feeling they can do anything. Not a blind "you've got this," but a book world that helps them feel brave enough to pursue it and realise it's true.
- **Self-audacity, with the proud, nurturing cheerleader, wrapped in a bow of wonder and whimsy.**
- A love letter to everyone who always felt too much for this world and their own lived reality.
- The spirit of it: someone who never put reality onto the situation, just genuinely cheered you on, asked about it, and said "go on, you've got this, make it happen."
- **It answers the villain directly.** He believes humans don't have the capacity to be bigger. The series ends by proving they do.

**A second theme: find out more about your loved ones.** They might be even more like you than you think.

**A third theme: we find the support system within ourselves and our chosen families in the end.** Not every parent becomes who you needed, and acceptance can be its own happy ending.

**A fourth theme: the scariest villain of all is the naive one.** The world ended because people naively let AI take the reins without the full picture. AI is just a mirror to humanity and to whoever controls it. The prison is the mind, not the circumstance. The true villain is when we limit ourselves based on fears and the unknown, and assume we have the answers.

**A fifth theme: every generation gets it wrong for the next one,** because each is stuck in its own collective experience. But all generations exist at the same time, so the world isn't about building it for one era, but for all eras to collectively exist.

- The realm councils, made up of all ages to represent all eras and needs, are the system's attempt at exactly this. The Director's frozen "this works, so it stays" overrides it.

### The argument the world is built on

- **The villain's answer (no):** The world can't handle the truth of reality, so it must be controlled. Humans can't be bigger. Everyone secretly loves struggle, and without it they'll create new struggle, so it's a duty to stop it getting as ugly as it can be. Given true free will, humans will always choose destruction.
- **The hero's answer (yes), via a real-world analogy, not a story event:** The father who believed "gay is bad" meets his son as he really is, meets his friends, admits he didn't know as much as he thought, and ends up waving a flag at Pride. People given truth and love can grow bigger than they were.
- **Why it's genuinely hard:** Truth costs something. Coming out can mean losing family or a career, so some people choose to lose themselves instead. Changing your mind means humility, grace, shame and backtracking, especially when the old belief was part of your identity. Some people want the whole world; some just want their little corner of it. Both are true.

### Smaller questions (future books or spin-offs)

- What happens after the truth comes out?
- What's more important: being true to yourself, or being accepted?
- What if you really had everything you ever wanted?
- Can you trust that honesty really is the best policy?

### Threads running through everything

- **Two things can be true.** Loving opulence, shiny things and innovation while hating the system that makes them. Loving screen freedom while grieving lost community.
- **Wonder against grey.** The enemy isn't darkness, it's people choosing boring and "realistic" over magic and beauty.
- **Minds, not guns.** War fought through law, persuasion, narrative and spectacle. Change without violence, without the cycle of getting worse again.
- **Depth against surface.** Patterns, mysteries, critical thinking and passionate nerds versus headlines, propaganda and performance.
- **Nobody's pawn.** Autonomy and free will. A hero who doesn't realise her impact, but refuses to be used by either side.
- **Who gets to name you.** A name you choose is freedom; a name stamped on you is a cage. Labels policed by people who've never lived them.

### Villain bar

Talented, calculated, unpredictable and genuinely convinced. Wages war without physical violence. All show, smoke and mirrors, like Snow. Believes they're right, like Thanos. Never telegraphed, unlike Coin.

### Raw material

**Obsessions:** gold and white Greek/Italian villas, grass and topiary, New York, Italy, conspiracies, patterns, solving mysteries, winning cases, marshmallow textures, pastels, collective wanting, money and material beauty as blessing and curse, nostalgia, neon and gaming aesthetics, cosy autumn, optimistic high-tech futures, Old Valyria, faerie woodlands, white marble opulence, 1800s World's Fairs, innovation, expansion, unapologetically whimsical people.

**Rage:** everything being depressing; people trapped by where they grew up; no critical thinking; performative identity; tearing each other down; made-up money systems; hypocrisy; war and violence; the patriarchy; lost community; black-and-white thinking; people feeling left out; the rat race; no true free will; no one looking up at the sky; choosing grey over beauty.

**Portals:** the Beast's grand library; Ready Player One's world and hidden hunt; Hogwarts as a place of learning; a city that never sleeps next to a quiet, tech-free garden of fairy lights and topiary; Japanese whimsy and side quests; vending machines with random things in them; karaoke next to a hula-rollerblading bar; marvelling at the universe, then marvelling at the human mind.

**The Feeling at the end of Book One:** enraged, awakened, needing to know more, satisfied but shaken, and "it's ON."

## Module 2: The Crack

**The Crack: our world, rearranged by a catastrophe humans caused and then rebranded as a miracle.**

- **The Real:** our world, rearranged. Real places, cultures and landmarks fused into one landmass, within living memory. The grandmother remembers the world before; the mother remembers the aftermath (or was too young, or never speaks of it); the protagonist was born after.
- **The Bent:** familiar, but not where it's supposed to be. A national park with purple trees, glowing bioluminescent landscapes, a city whose transport runs in the sky.
- **The Secret:** people were told an undersea climate catastrophe beyond human control nearly ended everything, and that humanity survived thanks to a divine fix. Officially it's called **The Great Reconnecting**. In truth, the Director's family's defence company ran an AI-controlled weapons test that went rogue, detonating two enormous bombs under the ocean on opposite sides of the globe and shifting the tectonic plates.

**Full detail of the event** (the family company's AI-run test, the two blasts, Isola and Trovata) lives in Module 6.

### Why it works

- **Blame spread thin.** Climate change is everyone's fault and nobody's, so the people who pulled the trigger hide inside collective guilt.
- **Guilt turned into gratitude.** Grateful people don't ask who did it, and questioning a divine fix feels close to blasphemy. Control through comfort, not force.
- **The Heart Statement, built into the map.** Every terrain, culture and city is now on everyone's doorstep. If the whole world is within reach, would you still look beyond it?
- **Oceans are gone, walls in heads remain.** The merge removed physical separation, but having everything nearby may stop people looking further.
- **A generational before and after.** For anyone old enough to remember, nostalgia is literal: their old world physically no longer exists.
- **A villain origin.** Anyone who saw what humans did that day could conclude humans can't be trusted with freedom.
- **Beauty from a wound.** A human-made disaster left purple forests and glowing landscapes behind.

### Visual rule

Familiar, but not where it's supposed to be. Film in real places and bend them through colour, lighting and practical effects, so fans can visit real locations and a theme park can follow the same rule.

### Three generations, three relationships with the truth

The grandmother remembers. The mother stays silent. The protagonist inherits a world she never saw being made.

### Parked

- Alternative cause: an outside force merged the world for humanity's own good. Kept as a possible in-world belief, or a future layer.
- Fun real-world anchor: scientists genuinely project future supercontinents (Pangaea Ultima, Amasia, Novopangaea).

## Module 3: The Rules

**In progress.** The world's "magic system" may be its economy and its systems rather than spells.

### What the Great Reconnecting changed

- Cultures, innovations, lives and livelihoods blended, and every system the modern world relied on changed.
- The world reorganised into regions defined by the unique skills and offerings of their land and surviving people, not by dictators.
- Most old-world wealth was poured into rebuilding, so old money largely evaporated.

### Economy

**The bands are called LiFE bands.** Their new features (Recalibrate) are rolled out through a beta that people sign up for.

**Decided: the update is branded REcalibrate** in all product marketing, a quirky tech capitalisation to match LiFE that quietly spotlights the "Re-" pattern. Institutions (the Reconnecting, Realignment, Rehoming) stay written normally.

- **Real-world marketing hook:** an in-world advert inviting people to join the LiFE beta doubles as the sign-up for the book series' newsletter and updates. A tongue-in-cheek hint at the twist: readers signing up are doing exactly what the protagonist's best friend did.

* Everyone wears a Fitbit-style bracelet that holds their digital wallet and points, and pays for everything, vending machines included. Its main function is points collection and smartwatch features, but it projects its display out into the air: no screens. It removed the screen and made the digital world physical, so you can watch everything and stay tapped in. (Real anchor: projected wearables such as the short-lived Humane AI Pin, which beamed its display onto your palm.)
* Points work like in-game currency in an MMO such as Albion Online: earned in various ways, or boosted through microtransactions paid with your past or your data, since money no longer exists.
* Because old-world wealth went into rebuilding, pay-to-win isn't a thing yet. Only now are point-rich people starting to appear.
* **Points are earned by completing community-based tasks.** The old world rewarded whoever shouted the loudest hate; this one rewards whoever does the most. But it's all toxic community: performative unity, goodness as a competition.
* Dropped: knowledge as currency.

### Basic rights, careers and bonus points

- **Everything you deserve as a basic right is free:** a home, good nutritious food, basic clothing, healthcare, learning, travel. Not everything is free, but every right is.
- **Every career pays a basic living wage, to everyone.** Bonus points come from excellent innovation and continued growth in your chosen career, plus accolades in Neuropa for all realms, such as speaking events and broadcasts.
- **No one works a job for the points,** so the real stakes are finding what you love. There's no "it pays well but I hate it."
- **Points are bonus fun:** the task boards' side quests pay for life upgrades and add-ons.
- **Weekly unlocks:** for example, 1,000 points opens a family's "Italian week" on the food menu, instead of the standard food: good and nutritious, but bland and always the same. Points also unlock house upgrades, more on-trend home developments, and the latest clothes for your realm.
- **Real anchor:** the idea of "universal basic services," where essentials like housing, food and transport are guaranteed for all.
- **Two things true:** no one goes without. But the basics are grey, and colour, flavour and wonder are paid upgrades. The grey-versus-wonder fight, built into the shopping menu.
- **A quiet irony:** families spend 1,000 points to unlock an "Italian week" menu, while Nonna cooks the real thing for free.
- **Parentification softens but stays:** children's points now fund the family's upgrades and treats, not its survival.

### The points game in everyday life

It works exactly like the world works now. **Depth is the true point scorer, but it isn't rewarded,** because the focus is on having more points, all in the name of "community and exploration."

- **The anxious:** "Do I have enough points for this trip out?"
- **The competitive:** "Nancy got more points helping the seam this month than me. I need to make double the cookies for the kids, there's no way she's winning."
- **The indifferent rich:** thousands of points, "what's the point lol," zero depth behind any of them.
- **The success stories:** some people have points and it genuinely shows the system working. Not everyone with points is bad, not everyone is broke, not everyone is rich.
- **The truly rich:** those who know exactly the life they want are the richest of them all.

### Leaderboards and vices

- **Leaderboards are unveiled through the bracelet** as projected pop-ups: constant, ambient social comparison. (Real anchors: Duolingo's leagues and streaks, Strava's rankings.)
- **Human vices survive inside the points system, in disguise.** No casinos, but mystery boxes and trading-card pack openings work as gambling addiction.
- **Real anchors:** video game loot boxes, which Belgium restricted as gambling; the trading-card pack-opening craze.
- The duality: a world that "removed" greed still sells the slot-machine feeling, in cute packaging.

### What points buy

- **Everything:** commodities, virtual gifts for friends, access, and community-related choices.
- **Prison commissary vibes:** goods and privileges bought from a single approved supplier, at prices the institution sets.
- **The underside:** the whole world's economy works like a prison shop. The Academy (and the company behind it) is the only supplier, and can nudge behaviour by changing prices.
- **Real anchor for virtual gifts:** livestream gifts bought with in-app coins.
- **Access as class:** point-rich people buy better seats, front rows and first picks.

### Pledging points to community needs

- **Points can't buy a bigger say.** Instead, people pledge points to vote for the predetermined needs of the community and the need for each job.
- Pledges also raise the points value of the job. If everyone really wants a new community centre, the pledges show the collective need, and make the job more valuable for the people who help it happen faster.
- **Real anchor:** participatory budgeting, where residents vote on how public money is spent, pioneered in Porto Alegre, Brazil.
- **The underside:** the options are predetermined by the councils, so the menu is curated. Pledges are capped per vote, so the point-rich can't simply outspend everyone. But the top 5 contributors each week get an extra 50 points to pledge, so the most active doers gain a little more say: fairer than wealth, but still tilted towards whoever performs the most tasks.

### Points by age

| Age | Personal | To family | Trust |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Under 16 | 10% allowance for treats | 40% | 50% to the Academy (no children's trust) |
| 16–20 (after attending the Academy) | 50%, with no obligation to share with family | 0% | 50%, unlocked at 21 |
| 21 and over | 100%, full autonomy | Optional | None |

**The family's 40% is discretionary.** Families don't have to spend it. They can let their kids keep it all, or put it in the family pot based on need. How a family handles it reveals what kind of parents they are, and kids always know which families "take."

**Update: no children's trust.** The under-16 share that went into a trust now goes to the Academy instead, paying for the Academy year, graduations, gowns and celebrations. Every point a child earns also counts towards their hidden leaderboard total, revealed at 16.

- **Real anchor:** California's Coogan Law, which requires part of a child performer's earnings to be held in trust until adulthood.
- **A financial cliff at 16:** when a child turns 16 and leaves for the Academy year, the family loses their 40% contribution. Families depend on younger children, which quietly incentivises having more of them.
- In 2077, the protagonist's younger brother becomes the family's main child earner the moment she turns 16.

### Paused trusts and the community pot

- **If a teenager is sent to Realignment, their trust is paused,** and assumed to resume once they realign into their chosen "right life path."
- **No one knows what happens to the points of people who die without family to inherit them.** A deliberate mystery.
- **Most points funnel back into the community pot,** which each realm uses to make its Thanksmas as amazing as it likes. The stronger the community, the more collective points, and the bigger the celebration.
- **Real anchor:** towns competing over Christmas light displays, like the Blackpool Illuminations.
- **The underside, for theorists:** if unclaimed points flow into the pot, realms may be decorating Thanksmas, lanterns and all, with the points of the disappeared.

### No fines, no penalties

- **Points are only ever lost by spending them,** and spending is encouraged.
- The shame of money is never dangled over anyone. Do a bad job or don't show up, and you simply don't get the points.
- **Real anchor:** loss aversion, the finding that losing something feels roughly twice as bad as gaining the same thing. By never taking points away, the system always feels kind.
- **The underside:** the economy has no stick, because the only real penalty is being sent away.

### Points expire yearly

- **No hoarding:** points can't be stockpiled across years, which makes it hard for dynasties to form.
- **Real anchors:** "use it or lose it" allowances, and Silvio Gesell's idea of money that loses value over time to keep it circulating, trialled in the Austrian town of Wörgl in the 1930s.
- **Trusts are exempt from expiry. The emerging point-rich class grows by converting points into lasting access, status or possessions, and by using their starter trusts wisely**.

* **The trust is the only time points can be hoarded:** every adult has the right to a "starter trust" at 21, to get them whatever they choose.
* **The trap:** the world is a dopamine, enjoy-everything, never-go-without, FOMO land. Shiny distractions are everywhere, so many starter trusts are blown on temptation, and the points flow straight back into the system.
* **Real anchor:** sudden-wealth stories, such as lottery winners or young heirs who spend a windfall quickly.
* The 21st birthday becomes an enormous milestone: the day the trust unlocks.

### Housing

- **A home is a right for every family.** Everyone has the basics.
- How a home evolves, and what's inside it, depends on points. The home of your dreams is within reach if you earn enough.
- Not everyone wants a mansion, and not everyone wants simplicity: homes reflect who people are.
- **Real anchors:** Vienna's large-scale social housing, and Finland's Housing First approach to homelessness.
- **The underside:** if the Academy provides every home, the Academy can also take it away, which is what "Rehoming" quietly means. And upgraded homes become visible scoreboards for keeping up with the Joneses.

* **Single young adults** can opt for one-bedroom studios or dorms to experience adulthood until they meet a partner.
* **Only families can opt for homes,** with one bedroom per person.
* **Couples count as a family,** like a Covid bubble, so they can share two-bedroom flats or dorms.
* **Real anchor:** social housing allocated by household size and bedroom need, as in the UK.
* **The underside:** coupling up and having children is how you get more space, a quiet push towards family life. People who live alone stay small, again.

- **Chosen families don't count as families,** unless friends have been collectively single for more than six years. Then they can opt for shared housing, though it's rare, because the single complexes just work better.
- **The underside:** the system only recognises blood and romance. The chosen family at the heart of the series' ending is something the system won't officially see.
- **Social judgement: "eternal spinsters" is shorthand for the vibe, not an in-world name. No official label yet, but that's how these people are unofficially judge**d.

### Healthcare

- **Out-of-this-world, high-tech bio medicine.** Pop-up robot healthcare comes to you, delivering treatments in packages, or nurses arrive on special hover-hospitals wherever you are.
- **Never fully explained,** like the miracle burn-healing gel sent to tributes in the Hunger Games.
- The protagonist doesn't fully understand it and isn't very exposed to it.
- **Andongo's medicine and biotech hub** likely drives it, possibly drawing on the deep-sea biology behind the purple flora.
- **The sky heals:** hovercraft, descended from war machines, double as flying hospitals.
- **The underside:** the bracelet's hidden meter measures radiation exposure, never health, and no one knows it's there.

* **No illness caused by the Reconnecting can be treated.** That's why everything else has become so streamlined: the system is constantly trying to fix those issues without admitting the truth.
* **The lie blocks the science.** Researchers can't name radiation without proving the Reconnecting was a nuclear blast, so the one thing medicine can't cure is the thing it most needs to.
* **Real anchor:** after Chernobyl, Soviet authorities restricted doctors from linking illnesses to radiation, recording vaguer diagnoses instead.

- **The illnesses have started seeping into the mainland.**
- **The Director is flapping:** he's desperate to find more innovators and genius kids to find the final cure, because everyone else is failing and he needs out-of-the-box thinkers.
- **The Exhibition's hidden purpose:** behind "Youth Innovation Shapes a Brighter Tomorrow," he's hunting for the mind that can cure what he can't name.
- **The Director's dilemma:** the curious, questioning, out-of-the-box thinkers he needs are exactly the people his system removes.
- **Two reasons he sees the protagonist:** as a threat, and as a hope.

**Decided: the bracelets secretly track radiation.** Beneath the points, projections and leaderboards, every bracelet is a radiation monitor.

- **Real anchor:** nuclear workers wear dosimeter badges that measure their radiation exposure.
- **Why it works:** it explains why everyone must wear one, why the bracelet must be worn at all times, and why the bracelets arrived in 2035, just as the Settling ended and the illnesses began.
- The points system becomes the perfect cover: the reason everyone happily wears a radiation monitor every day.

### How a task works

1. **The councils decide the tasks.**
2. **People bid on tasks,** eBay-style.
3. **Clock in with the band.** It automatically logs the task being done and completed.
4. **The Academy reviews the job.** Points are released once it's confirmed complete.
5. No points are deducted for time taken or finer details. If the job isn't complete, the points are rejected, so there's never any benefit to faking it.

- **The underside:** the band logging every task means the Academy sees exactly where you are and what you're doing while you work. And "complete" rewards the minimum that passes, never depth.

### Bidding: like gig tickets

- **Jobs are automatically allocated every day,** so everyone has baseline work.
- **The best tasks are released like gig tickets.** People race to secure the fun ones, the ones worth the most points, and the ones in the places or roles they want.
- **Families compete as teams:** more family members with their bands open can secure more jobs for the household each day.
- **Real anchor:** the scramble for concert tickets, such as the queues that crashed during the Eras Tour sales.
- **The underside:** bigger households win; people living alone, the elderly and small families lose out. One band per person means no bots, just numbers.
- A daily ritual: the moment tasks drop, every wrist in the world lights up.

### The daily drops

- **7–8am: the under-16s drop.** Community-focused, learning-based and points-heavy tasks. Thanks to the generous "learning element" of the School of Life, kids' points largely fund their families' upgrades and treats.
- **9–10am: the adult market.** A daily scramble, like the rush to get a doctor's appointment at 8am in real life.
- **One automatic task each day for everyone.** No one is above doing their bit; it varies, but everyone helps the realm's running order at least once a day.
- **The underside: the economy parentifies children by design.** Families' upgrades depend on their kids' points, so the protagonist's parentification is systemic, not just personal. A family's fortunes ride on its children, and on their Exhibition placements.
- Two things true: kids feel valued and learn constantly, and they carry a weight no child should.

* **The Director does a daily task too,** across the realms. A real people person, his task is always in the learning section, with the kids.
* **The underside:** he personally shapes the generation that will inherit his world, scouts talent, and is perfectly placed to notice children who don't quite fit. It's part of why he deeply sees the protagonist.
* **Real anchor:** leaders and royals visiting schools as beloved photo opportunities.

### Emotional depth and neglect

- Does someone even want help? Are you actually helping them, or helping because you need to hit 10 points while, deep down, they're emotionally neglected?
- A big theme: emotional depth, neglect, and truly knowing thy neighbour. Not just "hey Jan!"
- Knowing that if Jan hasn't come outside for weeks, she'd never miss **her weekly routine**, so something must be wrong. Or that if she didn't wave, when she always believes a wave and a smile make a good day, something is wrong.
- Connects to the Heart: like the Pride dad, real change comes from knowing a person, not a label, a headline or a points total.
- Named principle: Goodhart's law. When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Points measure doing, not caring.

### Education: the School of Life

- Schools become a "School of Life." The points system includes basic tasks that help young people grow into functional adults.
- **Points determine your overall place in society.** A kid who's super helpful and nurturing naturally excels towards health jobs; one who's good with numbers becomes an accountant.
- **No single test.** Unlike the aptitude test in *Divergent* or the Ceremony of Twelve in *The Giver*, the "test" is a whole childhood of behaviour, tracked by the bracelet.
- **Tension with choice.** Rehoming is meant to be a choice, and people are meant to name themselves. If points decide your place, the system is naming you.
- **Two things true:** for many, the matching genuinely works. For a girl who feels nothing doing tasks, her profile may point nowhere, or towards Realignment.

### The Exhibition

**The world's defining ritual.** A grand, World's Fair-style event in marble pavilions, broadcast across all three Continuents: the final exam of the Academy year, with pomp, music and a speech from the Director.

- Hunger Games-level spectacle, with no one dying: the stakes are social and emotional.
- Neuropa, home of media, would likely produce the broadcast.
- **A dark undercurrent from real history:** historic World's Fairs celebrated innovation, but some also put people on display as exhibits. Celebration and judgement in the same marble hall.
- It opens on Reconnection Day, honouring the rebuild heroes and the Seven.

### How it works

- Like a debutante's coming out to society crossed with choosing a university path.
- **Every 16-year-old must submit something** to the Exhibition, for growth and innovation in their passionate field. The official belief: the youngest minds are the most innovative and untainted.
- The bands (bracelets) and the points system help young people realise what they're naturally good at.
- Submissions are put forward in their area's top categories. Each region is a hub for a type of innovator that keeps society functioning and moving with passion (as LA is the home of triple-threat child stars, while occasionally someone from elsewhere shines and becomes an A-list Oscar winner).
- **The Exhibition is the true potential-finding moment that turns people into legacies.**

### The Exhibition season

### The Grand Academy competition

- **Every child enters at 15,** and hidden scores decide who's chosen. Some test well and others don't, so selection rests on effort and community contribution.
- **Four students from every realm, 40 in total,** are chosen for the Grand Academy, the televised flagship. Everyone else attends a realm campus. Winners receive the opportunity to become Fellows.
- **Cross-realm houses:** at the Compass Ceremony, the 40 are sorted into four houses of ten, each with one student from every realm. They rise or fall together.
- **Why it works:** collaboration and community are core values, every house blends all ten realm specialities, and real breakthroughs often come from mixing disciplines, which quietly serves the Director's hunt for a cure.
- Her Four could be in her house.

* **Houses are formed at the Compass Ceremony** that opens the Academy year: the world's equivalent of the Sorting Hat, and the series' identity hook.

### The Compass Ceremony

- **Each student touches a giant golden compass,** the Academy's symbol. It spins and points to one of the four houses, each embodying one of the Academy's four rules.
- **Two layers of identity:** your realm is where you're from, carrying its North; your house is who you are.
- **The fandom question:** "What's your house?"

**The ten Norths:**

**Decided: the ten Norths are each realm's individual ethos,** not personal values. The Compass sorts students only into the four groups.

1. **Truth** (inspired by Neuropa): *In veritate lux*, "In truth, there is light."
2. **Boldness** (Cariberia): *Fortes fortuna adiuvat*, "Fortune favours the bold."
3. **Play** (Shangokyo): *Ludendo discimus*, "Through play, we learn."
4. **Wonder** (Mediterrania): *Natura artis magistra*, "Nature is the teacher of art."
5. **Justice** (Scandinada): *Fiat iustitia*, "Let justice be done."
6. **Wisdom** (Himalindia): *Nosce te ipsum*, "Know thyself."
7. **Hope** (Safarabia): *Dum spiro spero*, "While I breathe, I hope."
8. **Vitality** (Andongo): *Carpe diem*, "Seize the day."
9. **Aspiration** (Persovia): *Ad astra per aspera*, "To the stars, through hardship."

### Population and how realms run

- **World population: about** 1.5 billion, mirroring the world's population in the golden age of the World's Fairs. Roughly 150 million per realm on average. Neuropa, the capital, may be larger.
- **AI and robotics run the heavy background:** energy, food production, water, transport maintenance and major construction.

### Regional dualities (the Exhibition hubs)

- **Neuropa:** media and storytelling vs academic prestige. Propaganda versus factual history.
- **Cariberia:** spectacle (film, stage, beauty) vs sport. The scripted versus the unscripted.

- **Mediterrania:** antiquity and faith vs fashion and design. The eternal versus the seasonal.

### Isola: the exile island and Realignment continent

- One final landmass that didn't connect to the blended world, used for Realignment.
- **An anomaly, not anyone's home.** A random piece of land that shouldn't be separate but is, so no real country or culture feels turned into the leftover world.
- **The irony:** in a world defined by reconnecting, the punishment is disconnection.
- **Real precedents:** Alcatraz, Robben Island, Devil's Island, Napoleon on St Helena, Australia as a penal colony.
- **Real geology:** microcontinents exist, such as Zealandia, a mostly submerged continent off New Zealand.

* **Origin: seabed that rose from the ocean during the event,** the immediate response to the bombs that actually caused the Reconnecting.
* **The scar.** It's the real-life reminder, and physical proof, that it wasn't an accident.
* **Hiding evidence in plain sight.** The government turned the proof into a prison. Nobody visits, and those sent there don't come back.
* **The cruellest irony:** the people most likely to question the official story are sent to the one place that proves them right, where no one can hear them.
* **Visual potential:** a raised ocean floor of trenches, coral, shipwrecks and deep-sea life. Possibly the source of the world's bioluminescence and purple flora (ties to Module 3).
* **Dots readers will connect:** the missing sister, and the protagonist's fear of Realignment.

### What it looks like

- **The brief:** a mix of the Upside Down and Maleficent's realm. Equally beautiful, eerie and mysterious, like a jungle. Inspiration only; the details must be original.
- **A seabed jungle.** What grows on a risen ocean floor: coral cliffs, kelp hardened into trees, glowing fungi, whale bones and shipwreck hulls as ruins.
- **Real deep-sea features to borrow:** marine snow (organic particles constantly drifting down in the deep ocean, an eerie real cousin of the Upside Down's floating spores), hydrothermal vents steaming, brine pools that look like lakes underwater.
- **Paradise and prison.** Soft, spa-like Realignment buildings set inside the eerie jungle.

### Arriving

- Arrivals are amazed by the beauty, and shocked it isn't weird, grey or bland as they expected.
- They're almost enamoured by how free it feels.
- They're also unsure and confused: why is the most unhinged, mysterious manifestation of the world they know the home of Realignment?
- **The inversion:** the exile island feels freer than the society that sent them there. Wonder lives where the "broken" are sent, while the content majority live with the grey kept out of sight.

### What the island really is

- **A place for the weird and wonderful:** the non-conforming parts of the world that didn't easily slot together.
- Where being misunderstood doesn't need to be boxed, or even understood. It just is.
- **Beauty in the eye of the beholder.** Those who see it as beautiful see it that way because it is. Those who see it as a prison find it weird, creepy, unnatural and gross.
- The island is the series' love letter to everyone who felt too much for this world, made into a place.
- No labels: the answer to "who gets to name you?" is no one.

### Why nobody really knows what it's like

- **People rarely return to where they came from.** Nobody leaves the place they were hurting in only to go back unchanged. They either stay on the island forever, or genuinely realign and find a place that works for them.
- **Every journey is unique,** a bit like ayahuasca: no one can ever truly tell you what it's like.
- **The mystery protects itself.** Someone who came from the island might seem jolly, weird or completely themselves, but you never knew who they were before, so you can't compare. And the people you did know who went usually never come back to your place, so you never hear their version.
- So rumours fill the gap. Fear of Realignment runs on stories, not facts.
- **Franchise engine:** every character's island journey is its own story, ideal for an anthology series.

### Its name: Isola

- Italian for "island," and the root of "isolation," so it sounds lonely and ominous, while simply meaning island.
- A quiet nod to Nonna.

### Trovata: the lost and found continent

- **The second blast site,** and Isola's sister scar. Italian for "found."
- **Not hidden, just forgotten:** written off as uninhabitable newly risen volcanic land (toxic vents, unstable ground, harsh climate), so the new world built around it. The forgotten middle child of the world.
- **Still contaminated with radiation,** unlike Isola, which was cleaned up for show.
- **Unofficial and secret:** where the Director quietly sends people who know too much (the truth-tellers, his sister) and the people broken by testing on Isola.
- **He knows people live there and doesn't care:** to him it's a "messy drawer." To the people there, it's where they were found, and some have built a free life, at a cost written in their bodies.
- Full history and detail live in Module 6.

- Regions get the blended names, not cities, so every culture has a home. Old cities survive inside regions as districts (Boston lives in Neuropa).
- Names have a committee-made, "they tried to fix it" feel, from the same people who called the catastrophe the Great Reconnecting. Cheerful branding over grief.
- To avoid confusion, the main place in each realm simply takes the realm's name (no city portmanteaus like Londhatton). Smaller district names can still nod to old places.

#### Cariberia

*In progress.*

- **Look and feel:** LA and Las Vegas had a baby with Havana, dressed for Day of the Dead. Movie-magic glamour, desert neon and knowingly fake replicas, wrapped in Havana's colour and nightlife and the marigold-and-sugar-skull spectacle of Día de Muertos.
- **Charisma, talent and beauty are the most valued proposition.** In Cariberia, being somebody means being a celebrity, much as it does in today's world.
- **Fame pays in attention, not money.** With no money to earn, celebrity rewards itself: attention, notoriety and a following are the prize, just as clout is for today's celebrities.
- **Real anchor:** the attention economy. Economist Herbert Simon observed in 1971 that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention," making attention the scarce resource.
- **Underside: fame never comes without sacrifice.** Cariberia's famous always have to be "on." The public persona takes over, and the private self has nowhere to live. Even the realm's grief has to be performed: the one realm allowed a full day of mourning must mourn on camera.
- **Switching off happens underground.** Cariberians go "off" in underground Havana-style bars and contraband clubs, the realm's secret backstage.
- **Real anchor:** Prohibition-era Havana, where Americans flocked to bars such as El Floridita and Sloppy Joe's to drink what was banned at home.
- **What counts as contraband:** old-world products outside the Academy-approved range, such as cigarettes and tequila. Everything is provided, but only Academy-approved items, through the commissary. Not every old-world product is available to everyone all the time, so the clubs trade in what the official shelves leave out.

Unlike the others, it is also allowed a full day for all its dead, held on the traditional Day of the Dead dates, 1 and 2 November, rather than on Reconnection Day. It joins the world's remembrance by blending it with its own Day of the Dead, evolved into a new-world version that honours the old tradition rather than ignoring it. The catch: the day must be broadcast across the realm as a big show. That is why it is allowed. Grief that is also entertainment is grief the system can live with.

## Module 5: The Power

**In progress.** A hierarchy with a beloved figurehead at the top, councils beneath, and power that looks fair, open and bottom-up.

### The Director (the Snow character)

**Decided: the Director's name is Benedict Reverington.** (Katey, 3 Oct 2026.)

- People like a figurehead, even in religion, so the world has one overall leader, the Director.
- The main villain: the world's most non-political figure. A head of popular culture turned voice of the people.
- Anti-billionaire, anti-government, widely followed and respected for innovation and philanthropy in their time.
- Like Katniss stepping into Snow's role after the Hunger Games: the hero of the last story becomes the system of this one.
- **The Coin trap to avoid:** Coin is also a rebel who became the new tyrant, and she falls flat because readers see it coming. This villain must be genuinely good at a lot, genuinely beloved, genuinely convinced, and never telegraphed.

### His origin and manner

- From an old-money family that knew the true reason for the Reconnecting, and may have had a hand in the bad elements of the world before.
- A man of privilege who is "of the people" and says f the system. Cheeky, sometimes too honest.
- The phenomenon to draw on: a privileged outsider the public turns into a folk hero, because he seemed to see ordinary people's struggle.
- Uses the secret through family ties, but leverages it to genuinely help the world. He truly believes this is the best option.
- Humble and openly willing not to be in charge, always ready to hand over the reins. The people won't let him: "No, you! You're our god!" Manipulation and cult-like devotion keep him there.
- Unlike Coin, not military and not boring.

### Why it bites

- **The honest liar.** Radical honesty about small things is the perfect cover for the one enormous lie.
- **His thesis keeps proving itself.** Every time he offers freedom and the people refuse it, he's shown that humans won't choose freedom when they have everything they want.
- **Refusing power as a performance has history:** Julius Caesar declining the crown while the crowd roared, and Augustus and Tiberius making a show of not wanting power (historians call it *recusatio imperii*). Is his humility real, strategic, or both? Ideally readers never fully know.
- **A generational mirror.** His family knew the truth; the protagonist's grandmother remembers the world before.

**Principle: people only ever buy into something they see themselves in.** Public folk heroes form around shared grievance (healthcare, mistreatment of loved ones, corruption), not the person. The Director is a mirror: people project their own hurt onto him, and that's where his power comes from.

### His motive: atonement

- At the core, humans are just humans. Telling people they ruined the world themselves would have caused further destruction.
- So he took the reins and made the most of the catastrophe, using it to shift the world into a united, guided, "together" community. He truly believes it's what everyone, and the earth, needed to heal.
- Everything his family represented was hateful. His silence on the human error is his way of righting the wrongs his family caused through years of capitalism and unethical billionaire behaviour.
- Like Paris Hilton speaking out about Provo Canyon and ADHD: insanely privileged, and also sincerely using that platform for something that genuinely matters to him. Both are true.
- **The noble lie:** Plato's idea of a comforting falsehood rulers tell to keep society in harmony.
- **Neighbour to differentiate from:** Ozymandias in *Watchmen*, who fakes a catastrophe to unite humanity. This Director didn't cause the disaster; he inherited the guilt, kept the secret, and rules as a caretaker, not through one act.
- **Mixed motives:** his silence heals the world, and it also protects his family.

### Backstory and psychology

- His dad died. His mother was his hero: she married into a family of horrible but insanely rich people.
- An only child to an emotionally beaten-down, trapped mother. A bit of a mommy complex, which is where his empathy and sense of justice come from, and his complicated hatred of money, wealth, titles and unearned privilege.
- Born in today's era of capitalist and technological growth, so he also likes shiny things and escapism, and knows the nice parts of his privilege.
- **His charm:** he can sit with anyone and truly understand them, resonating emotionally in a way that's captivating.
- **His arc:** over the years, that understanding turns into paranoid control. He stunts natural evolution and change: "this works, so now it stays working," instead of "different eras need different things." The "in my day you'd be lucky to have this" argument every new generation hears.

**His mother:** still alive, but barely. He believes the truth coming out wouldn't only shame the bad side of the family, it would shame her too. He refuses to let the end of her life, or her parting legacy, be tied to the cruelty of the rich family name. By carrying the new order forward, he has reinvented the legacy to honour her rather than saving his father's family's name.

- **The inverse of the Pride dad.** There, a parent changed because of a child's truth. Here, a son hides the truth to protect a parent.
- **A ticking clock.** She is barely alive. Her death could free him to tell the truth, or harden him completely.

### Patterns worth noticing

- **The mirror.** He carries the same duality as the audience: loves the shiny things, hates the system. By the series' own principle, readers will see themselves in him.
- **Same gift, opposite use.** His superpower is truly knowing people, the exact depth the world lacks (noticing when Jan doesn't wave). The hero and villain may share a gift and differ in what they do with it.
- **The gilded cage repeats.** His mother was trapped in comfort and wealth. Without seeing it, he builds a gilded cage for the whole world, for its own safety.
- **Frozen time.** He becomes the older generation that won't let the new one in, and the protagonist was born after the Reconnecting.

### His mother's role

**His mother knows the truth** and is complicit in the lie. She's proud of her son and doesn't see the need to ever tell anyone if everyone's happy now and it's fixed. An under-the-rug kind of woman.

- She is the comfortable answer to the Heart Statement, in person: she has everything she wanted, so why would she want the truth?
- **Two silent mothers.** His mother stays quiet to protect a legacy; the protagonist's mother stays silent about the Reconnecting too.

### Open questions

- [ ] When he goes over the edge, what does his anger look like without guns or armies?

### Hierarchy

- The Director: figurehead and voice of the people.
- Realm councils: the peacekeepers and prime ministers of the world.

### Realm councils

- Each Continuent (Amerope, Afriopia, Asioralia) has a nominated council of all ages, representing all eras and needs.
- Every month, people across the realm submit tasks highlighting real-world community needs: more houses, more jobs, more care for the elderly, more parks, more school resources.
- The council streamlines the submissions into that month's task list, which people complete to earn points.

### How governance runs day to day

- **Each realm sends a representative** (like the protagonist's dad for Neuropa).
- **The ten representatives and the Director sit at the Academy's round table.**
- Continuent councils handle each Continuent's monthly task lists.
- **Task points values** are suggested by the AI from community-need data and approved by the councils.
- The Director chairs, guides and has the final say, while presenting himself as one voice among many.

### Design rule: never look like a dictatorship

- Permanent, constant proof throughout the series that this is not a dictatorship: votes, councils, choices, offers, public panels.
- **People really want it to be something they choose, but they also want someone to blame.**
- This can become the Director's angry rant when he snaps: you wanted the freedom to choose, and someone to blame when it hurt. Echoes Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, who argues people long to hand the burden of freedom to someone else.

### The true crime: secret testing on the sick

- **The bracelet is secretly an exposure meter.** Like a nuclear worker's dosimeter, it measures each person's radiation exposure: a number, not a diagnosis.
- **People whose readings climb too high are quietly removed** into Realignment on Isola, where new fixes are tested on them.
- **Those who respond well, or whose exposure was light, are returned to society in a different realm,** so they never tell loved ones anything strange and are never spotted as changed.
- **Those broken by the tests are discarded to Trovata.**
- **It explains the mystery:** why people rarely return to where they came from, why returners seem like strangers, and why rumours fill the gap.
- **The crux in one line:** people are quietly taken, tested on, and either relocated or discarded, while everyone is told they've been given a fresh start.
- **Real anchors:** the Tuskegee study, in which Black American men with syphilis were deceived and left untreated for decades; and the US human radiation experiments, revealed in the 1990s.

### The unforgivable line

- **He exiled the truth-tellers to the place he knew would slowly kill them.** In the early years, scientists, journalists and survivors who pieced together the truth were sent to Trovata, the still-contaminated second blast site. His sister too.
- **No cure exists for them, because honest research would mean naming radiation, which would prove the Reconnecting was a nuclear blast. The lie blocks the science that could save them, and he chooses the lie**.
- **Structural, not sadistic:** cruelty as the price of the peace he believes in.
- **Isola was cleaned up for show; Trovata was left poisoned** for the inconvenient.

### The real-world stakes

- **Who disappears, and where:** misfits and people with high exposure readings go to Isola for Realignment and testing; those who respond are relocated to a different realm; the broken are discarded to Trovata, alongside the truth-tellers.
- **The protagonist's danger:** her readings are normal, but her curiosity is not. If her questions are noticed, she becomes a truth-teller, and truth-tellers end up on Trovata.
- **The Exhibition** puts her in the world's spotlight for eleven weeks, where curiosity is hard to hide.
- **She must perform happiness every day.** The performativity she hates becomes her mask.
- Big Brother surveillance is built into the bracelet.

### Escalation: the mood-therapy update

- **The marketing: a mood-ring-style "peace" feature.** Your band glows with your mood and gently guides you back to calm, balance and your higher self. Therapy and inner peace, never illness.
- **What it really adds:** mapping and automatic location sharing, pitched as suggesting new areas for community projects. For the first time, bands quietly report where people are.
- **Real anchors:** 1970s mood rings; today's wellness and mindfulness features on smartwatches.

* **Announced nonchalantly at the beginning of Book One** as the latest marketing update, the kind of product news readers skim past.
* **Rolls out during the Grand Final,** while the whole world is watching.
* **Book One's climax is built in:** the eleven-week clock ends with every bracelet updating at once, at the moment the protagonist's anomaly is most likely to be exposed.

### How the cover-up held

- **Undersea cables:** most internet traffic between continents runs through seabed cables. The event severed them, causing a global communications blackout for weeks or months. Nobody could compare notes.
- **Radiation:** the blasts were deep underwater, and water absorbs much of the radiation. Contamination is concentrated at the two blast sites, Isola and Trovata, which is why both are restricted or "uninhabitable." Elevated readings are blamed on volcanic vents.
- **Data:** the family company ran much of the world's satellite and sensing infrastructure, so it controlled what was measured and published.
- **Scientists:** many died. Those who suspected were absorbed into the Academy, dismissed as conspiracy theorists in the chaos, or quietly moved to Trovata.

### How the merge happened

1. **The Shake (hours):** quakes, tsunamis, the blasts.
2. **The Drift (weeks to months):** the continents grinding together.
3. **The Settling (years):** aftershocks, new land rising, coastlines holding.

- **Losses:** around 80% of the world's population. From over 8 billion in 2027 to about 1.5 billion, roughly the world's population in the golden age of the great World's Fairs around 1900. Lost land is spread across every Continuent, so no single culture is erased.

### How nations became one world

- Most governments were wiped out alongside the Capitol. Survivors formed emergency coalitions by Continuent.
- **Armies became the Restoration Corp,** using military logistics for disaster relief.
- **Nuclear weapons were dismantled in public ceremonies:** the Director's swords-into-ploughshares moment.
- **Resistance** was resolved softly with resources, inclusion and points. The few who refused drifted to Trovata.
- **Old nations survive as cultural identities,** like districts within the realms.

### Language and communication

- Everyone keeps their home language. **Bracelets translate in real time.**
- Young people speak a blended slang mixing words from every culture.
- The internet was rebuilt as the Academy's network, on the company's technology.
- Neuropa produces most media and broadcasts, shown on public screens everywhere.

### Faith after the Reconnecting

- Religions thrived amid disbelief at having survived.
- **Openness between faiths:** people were more grateful to trust in their own faith than to tear down one another's.
- **Real anchor:** research has found that people tend to become more religious after major disasters such as earthquakes.
- **Two things true:** genuine comfort and interfaith grace, one of the things the new world truly got right, partly built on gratitude for a "divine fix" that was a human-made catastrophe.

### Era 6: The new systems

- **First, he abolished money,** declaring it "the root of all evil." For a while, it worked.
- Then everything went wrong, because a system needs an economy for order.
- **It was agreed the points system was better suited:** people rewarded for rebuilding the world, not for personal greed.

**The first Exhibition** was created out of the Director's passion for the 1800s World's Fairs, something he'd been vocal about the world needing more of before the Reconnecting.

* **A festival that slowly hardened into a judgement:** the first Exhibitions were celebrations; from 2051, they began deciding people's places.

- **Real anchor:** Nordic rehabilitation-focused prisons, such as Norway's, which have some of the lowest reoffending rates in the world. Fits Scandinada's law and justice speciality.

### Microtransactions without money

**Money is gone, but microtransactions stay.** Two ways to pay, both pitched as kindness:

- **Pay with your past (a newer model).** Trade in old-world belongings for points, like taking extra tesserae in the Hunger Games for more entries. Branded as decluttering; really, it's concealing the past and burning the books.
  - Every map, photo, journal and heirloom traded in erases a little more evidence of the world before.
  - A threat to everything Nonna kept.
- **Pay with your data.** Share data to help upgrade the technology and innovate, pitched as "your data is important, so you should be paid for it!"
  - Real anchor: genuine proposals for "data dividends" that pay people for their data.
  - Empowering pitch, surveillance outcome: more tracking through the bracelet, more fuel for the AI.

* **Neuropa is the "capital" zone, and the Director is based there.** People live and breathe the new world every day: more indoctrinated, eye-rolling, not questioning it.
* **Her family doesn't trade the past,** because they don't need to: her dad works for the Director, so they're points-secure. Nonna also knows better.
* **Her friends' families do,** trading old things for points to keep up with the Joneses.
* **Status, not starvation.** In the capital, the scheme runs on social pressure rather than desperation.
* **A privilege mirror:** like the Director, she's insulated by a father tied to the system, and still unhappy.
* Nonna's few old-world things are rare in the capital, which makes them precious, and risky.

### The Director's presence

- **Shrine mentality, not dictator.** The face of popular culture, the current national sweetheart, with Times Square-style modern media marketing.
- **Elusive, not oversaturated.** Always on the screens, but never on every channel.
- **The public mood:** "Oh my god, aren't we lucky he's here," and "Ahhh, look, he's here!"
- **Royal-style scarcity:** rare appearances make each one an event, like a royal balcony wave or walkabout.
- **Orwell inverted.** In *1984*, Big Brother is watching you from every wall. Here, everyone is watching for him, while the bracelet quietly does the watching.
- Everyone has the same opportunities: the protagonist submits to the Exhibition like every child in the world, dad or no dad.

### The Academy: the seat of power

- **The most beautiful, magical, celestial, grand building ever.** White marble and gold, domes and spires, cypress trees and topiary, waterfalls and reflecting pools, hover vehicles drifting past, grand libraries with ladders and glowing globes, a star-map dome over a round table, statues, banners, archways opening onto the sea.
- **Called the Academy:** a play on everyone eternally growing, learning and evolving.
- **It's the government building,** like a giant town hall.
- **The leader's title: "the Director"** (decided): not a ruler, a guide. Humility as branding.
- **The answer to a Module 1 question.** Beauty and abundance aren't the enemy or withheld: they're the seat of power. The author's dream aesthetic is home to the lie.
- **A third Plato echo:** Plato founded the original Academy in Athens, alongside Atlantis and the noble lie.
- The region of academic prestige houses the institution guarding history's biggest lie.
- A round table suggests equality, like Arthur's knights, while one man directs it.

### The Academy's symbol: a compass

- The official symbol is a **compass, for guidance**: a gold, eight-pointed compass star.
- **True north:** the Academy presents itself as the thing that points to truth.
- **The irony:** a compass is meant to be held by the traveller. Here, the Academy holds it and decides everyone's direction.
- Old-world maps carried compass roses too, so the symbol quietly links back to the world before.
- Merch: banners, robes, bracelet engravings, the inevitable fan tattoo.

### Visual references for the Exhibition (inspiration, not canon)

- **The mood:** students in black and white robes embroidered with the gold compass, presenting holograms, glowing globes, a holographic whale and a tree under glass; fireworks over the water at night; a grand amphitheatre stage.
- **Banner style:** grand, hopeful slogans, such as "Youth Innovation Shapes a Brighter Tomorrow."
- **Not canon:** the reference images' "Eight Reaches" banner and their eight category banners don't match the world (ten realms, four houses) and aren't used.

* **Terminology:** the ten areas are **realms** ("the Realm of Neuropa"); the three big groupings are **Continuents**. ("Reaches" was rejected.)

- **Why "Continuents" (decided 3 Oct 2026):** every continent merged, so although the world is now one landmass, the three groupings were renamed Continuents to honour them. A Continuent is a continuation of the continents that came before, but new.
- **No sea in the present day (decided 3 Oct 2026):** because the continents squeezed together, there are no open seas any more. The water around the Academy and Neuropa is a great man-made lake, so present-day descriptions say "lake", never "sea". The old oceans are only mentioned when describing the time before and during the Reconnecting.

### Era 1: The world before

- **The Director's family business:** a global defence contractor on the scale of Lockheed Martin. Everywhere, with deep government and military ties across every area of the world.
- **Fictional, built from archetypes** rather than any real company: the scale of modern defence giants, plus the family-dynasty structure of historic arms families such as Germany's Krupp.
- **Real anchor:** Eisenhower's 1961 warning about the "military-industrial complex."

* **What they were testing:** scientists had used AI to create a new type of nuclear weapon designed to neutralise another bomb with zero detonation. It relied on AI-run testing, embedded chips and advanced mechanics never tested before.

### Era 2: The event

- The test went rogue. With so little human control, the AI glitched or changed course midway.
- Instead of neutralising the test bomb, **two megalithic bombs detonated at the same time, on opposite sides of the globe, under the ocean.**

### Era 3: The immediate aftermath

- **Awful and world-changing.** It felt like the apocalypse: many deaths, rubble, clean-up, disaster and destruction.
- **And also so much humanity:** community, a sense of purpose, belonging and duty.
- **Real anchors:** Britain's "Blitz spirit," and research such as Rebecca Solnit's *A Paradise Built in Hell*, which documents how ordinary people spontaneously form generous communities after disasters.

### Eras 4–5: From rebuild to new order

- **The points system grew out of the aftermath feeling,** and out of a shared lesson: humanity really does thrive together.
- Lessons people already knew from our time: the camaraderie after wars and the pandemic, and the age-old "we have such fun as a family, shame we only see each other at weddings and funerals."
- **What always ruined it was "going back to normal":** jobs to do, rent to pay, the disaster cleaned up, the economy ready to boom again.
- **This time was a clean slate,** not a slight restructuring of culture.
- **A millennial consensus:** "We don't mourn the old times, because we never had a good time or hope. F the system that never kept its promises. Things have to change."
- The Director became the leader of that same message, and the world became what it is now.

### Why it bites

- **The pandemic promise kept.** In 2020, people said things would never go back to normal, and they did. In this world, they didn't.
- **The audience's generation built this world.** Nonna was 26 at the time of the event, so the audience's generations (Gen Z like Nonna, with millennials as the adults around her) are the grandparents of the story.
- **The cycle the series fears:** one generation's revolution has become the next generation's cage. Their "things have to change" has hardened into "this works, so it stays."

### How he became the Director

- **Before:** his family were basically the state and government of America, and its underbelly too. His **older brother**, widely liked, was in the running for US president.
- **The disaster wiped out the Capitol** with most of its people inside, and many world leaders died too. In the following weeks, many elderly people didn't survive illness, clean-up and the aftermath.
- **A power vacuum.** In the years after, he used resources, connections, his public likeness and shared grief (losing his brother and his father) to present himself as a human who was in it with everyone. He already had "the world's darling" energy.
- He'd been speaking out about the old system failing before the disaster, so when the dust settled and the world needed order, **the public cried out for a leader like him.**
- **He agrees once he realises what really happened,** offering to lead "while the world finds its feet," because he doesn't want the truth about his family to come out.
- **He actually does a good job.** He puts the bad tech to good use, the world begins to thrive, and it gradually takes the shape it has when the book opens.

### Why it bites

- **The spare becomes the king.** The younger son, never meant to lead, inherits the world.
- **The world's designated survivor.** Like the official the US keeps away from big gatherings in case of catastrophe, he's who's left.
- **Shared grief as a mirror.** Everyone lost someone; so did he. People buy into what they see themselves in.
- **Temporary that became permanent.** "Until the world finds its feet" has lasted 50 years. Emergency powers that never end are a pattern as old as Rome.

### Why it's strong

- **Topical fear.** AI anxiety is a defining worry for millennials and Gen Z. The real-world debate about keeping a "human in the loop" for weapons is the exact failure here.
- **A peace weapon caused the catastrophe.** A device built to prevent explosions caused the biggest one in history.
- **Father and son mirror.** The father's company engineered safety and caused ruin. The son engineers a safe, peaceful society. Will it end the same way?
- **The cover story is accidentally true.** People were told it was an event "out of human hands." It literally was: the AI was out of human hands.
- **Two blasts explain the merge** (the crust shoved from both sides) and the seabed rising into Isola.

### Trovata: the second blast site

- Like the lost city of Atlantis: a hidden landmass.
- Home to people who truly live off the grid of everything, like underground homeless networks or *Divergent*'s factionless. No bracelets, no points.
- **Two scars, two answers.** Isola is the scar the state weaponised: known, official, where people are sent. The secret continent is the scar the people claimed: unknown, unofficial, where people choose to go.
- **A Plato double:** Plato described Atlantis, and also proposed the noble lie the Director lives by.
- Differentiate from the factionless: these people aren't rejected leftovers, they've opted out.

* **Not hidden, just forgotten.** It was presumed uninhabitable, especially at the time of the Reconnecting, so it became the forgotten middle child of the new world.
* **Real anchors:** the Chernobyl exclusion zone, where wildlife flourished and some residents quietly returned; Bikini Atoll, a nuclear test site whose coral recovered.
* **A recurring pattern:** in this world, the overlooked is where power and truth hide: Nonna, her diary, Isola, and now a whole continent.

- **Official explanation:** not a "do not enter" zone. It couldn't be immediately built on or altered to fit the state, so the new world built around it, and then it simply wasn't needed. "More of a struggle" to live on, like a planet that looks uninhabitable.
- **Realistic causes:** newly risen volcanic land, with toxic gas vents, unstable ground and harsh climate. It fits the cover story of an "undersea event."
- **Real anchor:** Surtsey, an island that rose from the sea off Iceland in the 1960s, kept off-limits while scientists watched life colonise it.
- **Thematic twist:** the villain believes humans love struggle. Here, people freely choose a harder life over comfortable control.

### The Director's hiding place

- **The villain sends people here,** not to punish them, but to hide them away. **The missing sister could be here.**
- **Distinct from Isola.** Isola is official and public: everyone knows about Realignment. The forgotten continent is unofficial and secret: where the Director quietly hides people who know too much.
- Fits his character: he avoids violence. He hides rather than kills.
- **Possible irony:** he believes he's hiding people in a forgotten wasteland, while those hidden, plus any stranded survivors and refusers, may have built a free society there.
- Settler candidates still available: people stranded at sea when the seabed rose, defectors from the family company, people who refused bracelets, escapees from Realignment.

* **He knows people live there, and he doesn't care.** To him it's exile island. It's been so many years since he sent anyone, and no one has ever surfaced, so he barely thinks about it.
* **"A messy drawer"** hiding the stuff that could ruin the rest of the system. Never seen as a threat.
* **His complacency mirrors the protagonist's mother's:** both mistake things staying quiet for things being fine.
* **Hubris:** the man who freezes time hasn't checked the drawer in years. Messy drawers are exactly where lost things turn up.

### Its name: Trovata

- Italian for "found." It's the lost and found: the Director thinks it's a messy drawer of lost things; the people there know they've been found.
- Pairs with Isola: two Italian names for the two scars, both quietly echoing Nonna.

### The company today

- **It still exists, under the Director's whole societal mantle.**
- Before: a big family known for opulence, with their hands in everything for centuries.
- Now: **the core of the entire new world system, the founding hub of the new world.**
- **Reconciling it with his estrangement:** he despised his father's establishment, so he reinvented the company rather than inheriting it unchanged, the same way he reinvented the family legacy to honour his mother.
- **The irony:** the machine that broke the world now runs it.

* **Yes: the company's AI still runs the new world's systems.** The Director took the helm and used the tech for the new system.
* **His justification:** his evolution was to take war technology and genuinely make it function for society, not for war, government and the rich. That's why he believes he's in the right.
* **Swords into ploughshares,** with real precedent: the internet began as a military network, and GPS was built for the military.
* **The ticking bomb:** the same kind of AI that went rogue without human control now runs bracelets, points and Exhibition profiles.

- **The AI is not the villain.** It's a mirror to humanity and to whoever controls it. Under the father, it served war. Under the son, it serves a gentle, controlling vision of society.
- **The real danger is naivety:** the world ended because people with big tech naively let AI take the reins without the full picture. The Director believes good intentions make it safe.
- Avoids the "evil AI" cliché, such as Skynet, in favour of something more honest and more frightening.

### Alternative: the Director's prequel

The mother's death could move to a prequel, as the origin of his villain arc, like *The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes* does for Snow.

- **Version B:** his sister almost tells the truth, the stress kills their mother, he has his sister killed, and tyranny begins.
- Trade-off: moving her death to a prequel loses the main series' ticking clock and the hero's Book 3 guilt beat.

- The ambiguity keeps him arguable and keeps the minds-not-guns rule intact.
- An unanswered question is a franchise engine: readers will theorise for years about what happened to her, and whether she's still out there.

* **Hidden in plain sight.** The sister is occasionally mentioned in passing as part of the "X" family, like a past Hunger Games victor named in a list. No name, no acknowledgement of her importance to his arc, until the time comes.
* Technique to borrow: Regulus Black, glimpsed in passing on a family tapestry in *Order of the Phoenix* and later revealed as R.A.B. Rereaders get rewarded; theorists get something to chase.

### Reconnection Day

- **Date: 7 May.** The Great Reconnecting happened on 7 May 2027.
- **Both a memorial and a celebration.**
- **Five minutes of noise, not silence.** The new world believes grief is best shared and emotions must be felt, not suppressed. Every year, everyone makes noise for five minutes: playing music, shouting into the void, singing names, speaking loudly to the universe, to release grief or reach those in other dimensions who might hear them.
- **Real anchors:** Irish keening and southern Italian lament traditions of loud, sung mourning; New Orleans jazz funerals; Israel's Memorial Day flowing directly into Independence Day.
- **Two things true:** genuinely healing, and also the one sanctioned five minutes a year when a world that patches over its problems is allowed to be loud about pain.
- Timeline detail: Nonna discovered she was four months pregnant soon after, so the protagonist's mother was born around autumn 2027.
- In 2077 it marks the 50th anniversary: the golden jubilee of the lie.

* **Reconnection Day opens the eleven-week Exhibition season every year.**

- **Optics:** the capital putting its own realm in the hardest slot looks humble and fair, never like a dictatorship.

* **The Thanksmas draw:** every year during the Gratitude Parade, the Director selects each realm's week at random, live in front of the world.
* **Real anchors:** the televised World Cup draw; Eurovision's running-order draw in past years.
* **Fairness theatre:** a public random draw by the most trusted man alive is perfect visible proof of fairness, and perfect fuel for fans who suspect it isn't random.
* Links the two biggest events of the year: Thanksmas sets the order, Reconnection Day starts the season.

- **The draw is truly random.** In Book One (2077), Neuropa happens to draw the week before the final.
